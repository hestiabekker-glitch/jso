Myopia in Children: Why Eye Doctors Are Now Treating It Like a Disease (Not Just a Prescription)

By Jason Stander·25 Aug 2026·5 min read

If your child's eyesight seems to get a little worse every year, you're not imagining it — and you're not alone. Myopia (short-sightedness) in children is rising sharply around the world, and 2026 has brought a real shift in how optometrists think about it. It's no longer treated as "just needing stronger glasses every year." Leading eye care bodies, including the American Optometric Association, are now urging optometrists to manage myopia as a progressive eye condition that needs active control, not just correction.

For parents in Polokwane and across Limpopo, this matters more than it might seem.

Why a bigger prescription isn't the whole story

For decades, the standard response to a child's worsening eyesight was simple: stronger lenses, next check-up in a year, repeat. The problem is that this approach only corrects the symptom — blurry distance vision — while doing nothing to slow the underlying elongation of the eyeball that causes myopia to progress.

That distinction matters because higher levels of myopia are linked to an increased lifetime risk of more serious eye conditions later in life, including retinal detachment, myopic maculopathy and glaucoma. Managing myopia early isn't about vanity or convenience — it's about protecting your child's long-term eye health.

What's new in 2026: myopia control without contact lenses

One of the most encouraging developments this year is the wider availability of myopia-control spectacle lenses — everyday glasses engineered with a special lens design that has been clinically shown to slow the progression of myopia in children, rather than simply correcting it. Lenses in this category (such as the Stellest design highlighted at recent international optometry conferences) work by altering how light focuses at the peripheral retina, which appears to signal the eye to stop growing as quickly.

This is a significant development for South African families for a practical reason: it gives children — and parents — an effective option that doesn't require the daily discipline of contact lens wear or overnight orthokeratology. A child simply wears their glasses as normal, and the lens does the work in the background.

Combined with tools already well established in myopia management — such as low-dose atropine drops and specially designed contact lenses — optometrists now have a genuinely individualised toolkit. The right approach depends on your child's age, lifestyle, prescription and how quickly their eyes are changing.

Why South African kids are worth watching closely

Two local realities make myopia management especially relevant here in Limpopo. Screen time has climbed steadily for school-aged children, and sustained close-up focusing on phones, tablets and laptops is one of the recognised environmental contributors to myopia progression. At the same time, high outdoor UV exposure across the province makes it essential that any eyewear strategy — corrective or protective — accounts for both focusing demands and sun exposure, not just clarity of vision.

The good news is that outdoor time itself is protective. Research consistently shows that children who spend more time outdoors, in natural light, tend to show slower myopia progression — one more reason a properly fitted, comfortable pair of glasses matters, so kids actually want to wear them outside and at play, not just at a desk.

What this means for your child's next eye test

If your child already wears glasses and their prescription seems to climb every year, that pattern itself is worth flagging at their next visit — it's often the clearest sign that active myopia management, rather than a simple lens upgrade, is the more appropriate path.
